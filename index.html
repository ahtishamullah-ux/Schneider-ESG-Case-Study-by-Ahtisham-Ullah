A Case Study on Improving Sustainable Strategies and Performance Through Implementation of Digital Transformation and ESG in Schneider Electric

AU
Ahtisham Ullah
University of Salento (Università del Salento), Lecce, Italy
ahtisham.ullah@studenti.unisalento.it linkedin.com/in/ahtisham-ullah
Period: 2005 – 2025 Method: Qualitative Single Case

Abstract.  This case study investigates how Schneider Electric SE improved its sustainable strategies and corporate performance through the joint implementation of digital transformation and environmental, social and governance (ESG) programmes between 2005 and 2025. Using a qualitative single-case design built on annual reports, Schneider Sustainability Impact (SSI) disclosures, third-party ESG ratings and academic literature, the study traces the company's evolution from an early adopter of sustainability reporting to the only enterprise ever ranked first twice on the Corporate Knights Global 100 index (2021 and 2025). The analysis shows how the EcoStruxure Internet-of-Things architecture, industrial software, digital twins and lighthouse smart factories were deliberately coupled with a measurable ESG scorecard, linking executive pay and €2.7 billion of bank financing to sustainability outcomes. Findings indicate a measurable decoupling of commercial growth from environmental impact: record revenue of €38.2 billion in 2024 alongside an SSI score of 7.55/10, MSCI AAA status and industry-leading Sustainalytics placement. The study concludes that digital transformation functions as the measurement-and-optimisation engine of credible ESG strategy, and proposes a transferable sequence — instrument, integrate, incentivise, monetise — for incumbents pursuing sustainable performance.

Keywords: digital transformation; ESG integration; energy management; EcoStruxure; corporate sustainability; decarbonisation.

Section 01Introduction

Few industrial companies carry a two-century manufacturing legacy while simultaneously topping global sustainability league tables. Schneider Electric — founded in 1836 in Le Creusot, France — has done precisely that, repositioning itself from a steel and armaments manufacturer into the recognised leader in the digital transformation of energy management and automation.[9]

The twin pressures of climate regulation and the Fourth Industrial Revolution have forced incumbent industrials to reconcile profitability with planetary boundaries.[4] Yet many ESG programmes fail not for lack of ambition but for lack of instrumentation: emissions, energy intensity and workforce metrics remain locked in annual, manually compiled reports. Digital transformation — sensorisation, connectivity, cloud analytics and artificial intelligence — offers the missing plumbing, converting sustainability from a reporting exercise into a continuous, optimisable operation.[3][7]

This paper examines Schneider Electric as an exemplary case of that coupling over two decades, from its first sustainability barometer in 2005 to the close of its 2021–2025 Schneider Sustainability Impact (SSI) cycle. The study addresses three research questions:

RQ1 How has Schneider Electric operationalised digital transformation to advance its ESG objectives?

RQ2 To what extent has this integration improved both sustainability and financial performance?

RQ3 Which transferable lessons emerge for industrial incumbents pursuing sustainable strategies?

The case matters beyond one firm. Energy management sits at the chokepoint of decarbonisation: buildings, data centres, industry and infrastructure all consume the electricity that Schneider's equipment distributes and its software optimises. If digital-plus-ESG integration demonstrably improves performance here, the playbook plausibly generalises across the industrial economy.

Section 02Literature Review

2.1 Digital transformation as an operational lever

Digital transformation is most productively understood not as a technology programme but as a strategy programme. Matt, Hess and Benlian[2] show that successful firms formulate explicit digital transformation strategies spanning use of technology, value creation, structural change and finance, while Kane and colleagues[3] demonstrate across thousands of executives that digitally maturing organisations are distinguished by strategy and culture rather than by any particular toolset. Schwab's account of the Fourth Industrial Revolution[4] frames cyber-physical systems as dissolving the boundary between physical operations and data — precisely the dissolution that ESG instrumentation requires.

2.2 ESG and corporate performance

The financial materiality of ESG is now supported by unusually broad evidence. Friede, Busch and Bassen's[5] aggregation of more than 2,000 empirical studies finds that roughly 90% report a non-negative relationship between ESG and corporate financial performance. Eccles, Ioannou and Serafeim[6] show that firms voluntarily adopting substantive sustainability policies by 1993 subsequently outperformed matched peers in both stock-market and accounting terms, establishing that deep — rather than cosmetic — integration drives the effect.

2.3 The digital–ESG nexus

A newer stream explicitly links the two agendas. George, Merrill and Schillebeeckx[7] argue that digital technologies condition how firms can pursue sustainability objectives, enabling measurement, transparency and ecosystem coordination at scales previously impossible. However, firm-level longitudinal evidence connecting a specific digital architecture to audited ESG key performance indicators (KPIs) remains scarce. This study addresses that gap through a two-decade tracing of one company in which the digital and sustainability strategies were, by design, the same strategy.

Section 03Methodology & Research Workflow

The research adopts a qualitative, single instrumental case study following Yin.[1] Schneider Electric was selected through purposive, criterion sampling: it is an extreme and revelatory case — the only corporation to top the Corporate Knights Global 100 twice (2021 and 2025)[10] — and an information-rich one, with two decades of quantified sustainability disclosure.

Three evidence streams were triangulated: (i) primary corporate documents — full-year financial statements,[11] quarterly Schneider Sustainability Impact scorecards,[14] and strategy announcements;[8] (ii) independent third-party assessments — Corporate Knights, S&P Global CSA, EcoVadis, Moody's, MSCI and Sustainalytics ratings;[10] and (iii) academic literature on digital transformation and ESG performance.[2–7] Analysis proceeded by chronological process-tracing of the 2005–2025 period, coding initiatives against the classical E-S-G pillars, and confronting stated targets with independently rated outcomes.

Limitations are acknowledged in Section 9: disclosure-based sources risk favourable self-presentation, and a single case constrains statistical generalisation; analytic generalisation to theory is nonetheless appropriate.[1]

Section 04Case Context: Schneider Electric

Founded in 1836, Schneider Electric spent its first century in steel, heavy machinery and electrical equipment before decisively pivoting toward electricity management through acquisitions such as Square D, Merlin Gerin and APC. Today, headquartered in Rueil-Malmaison, France, the group employs more than 150,000 people across over 100 countries, describing itself as "the most local of global companies".[9] Its portfolio spans two reporting segments — Energy Management and Industrial Automation — serving homes, buildings, data centres, infrastructure and industry.

Strategically, the company frames itself as an "Impact Company": sustainability is positioned not as corporate social responsibility adjacent to the business but as the business model itself. Roughly 70% of revenue was already derived from sustainable solutions by 2021, with about 73% of investment directed toward them.[9] Leadership transitions — from Jean-Pascal Tricoire through Peter Herweck to Olivier Blum, appointed CEO in November 2024 — preserved strategic continuity, each executive having previously steered the sustainability agenda.[10]

"There is no magic formula to being repeatedly listed as a most sustainable company, it's about doing well and doing good." — Olivier Blum, then Chief Strategy & Sustainability Officer [10]

Section 05Digital Transformation Architecture

5.1 EcoStruxure: the IoT backbone

The centrepiece of Schneider's transformation is EcoStruxure, its open, interoperable, IoT-enabled architecture, re-architected in 2016 across three layers: connected products (breakers, drives, sensors and actuators with embedded intelligence), edge control (on-premise power and process control), and apps, analytics and services (cloud optimisation, remote monitoring and advisory). The platform is deployed across the group's own factories and sold to customers in buildings, data centres, grid infrastructure and industry — meaning Schneider tests its decarbonisation tooling on itself before monetising it, a practice the company calls being its own first customer.

5.2 Software scale-up: AVEVA, ETAP and digital twins

The group deepened its software stack through successive investments, culminating in the full acquisition of UK industrial software leader AVEVA, completed in January 2023, and of electrical digital-twin specialist ETAP. Combined with EcoStruxure, these assets let operators model energy flows digitally before changing them physically — simulating microgrids, predicting demand, and automating efficiency in data centres and plants. Artificial-intelligence capabilities have been progressively embedded, including a dedicated Chief AI Officer function and partnerships applying generative AI to resource-optimisation problems.

5.3 Smart factories as living laboratories

Internally, the Smart Factory programme digitised the group's ~200-site manufacturing footprint. Several plants — including Le Vaudreuil (France), Batam (Indonesia) and Hyderabad (India) — have been designated World Economic Forum Lighthouse factories, while Lexington (USA) and Wuxi (China) counted among the Forum's earliest Sustainability Lighthouses, a designation requiring quantified environmental gains from Fourth-Industrial-Revolution technologies.[16] These sites operationalise the paper's central mechanism: continuous telemetry converts sustainability KPIs into variables that engineers can tune daily rather than auditors can tally annually.

Section 06ESG Implementation

Schneider's ESG journey long predates the acronym. The company introduced its first sustainability barometer in 2005 — among the earliest of any industrial firm — reporting quantified targets on a quarterly cadence unusual even today.[8] As then-Chairman and CEO Jean-Pascal Tricoire noted, "when we introduced our first sustainability barometer in 2005, we were an early adopter of ESG matters."[9]

In January 2021 the barometer was superseded by the Schneider Sustainability Impact (SSI) 2021–2025: six long-term commitments translated into eleven global targets, supplemented — for the first time — by local targets set across the 100+ markets in which the group operates.[8] Climate commitments are validated against a 1.5°C trajectory: carbon-neutral operations by 2025 and net-zero across the entire value chain by 2050.

6.1 Governance and financial hard-wiring

What distinguishes the programme is enforcement architecture. Executive remuneration is explicitly linked to SSI performance and ESG ratings, and in 2022 the group signed €2.7 billion of bank facilities whose pricing is indexed to the annual SSI score — converting sustainability from narrative into covenant.[12] The effect registered externally: Corporate Knights specifically cited the pay-for-sustainability linkage in awarding Schneider first place in 2025.[10]

6.2 Ecosystem programmes

Recognising that most of its footprint sits outside its own walls, Schneider launched the Zero Carbon Project (April 2021) to help its top 1,000 suppliers halve operational emissions by 2025, and the Energize programme (October 2021) to open renewable-energy purchasing to the supply chains of ten global pharmaceutical companies.[13] Its Sustainability Business consulting arm sells the same decarbonisation method to external enterprises — productising the internal playbook.

Section 07Performance Outcomes

7.1 Environmental performance

The headline environmental result is decoupling: record commercial growth achieved while operational emissions fall. On the demand side, EcoStruxure-class solutions helped customers and suppliers save or avoid 381 million tonnes of CO₂ between 2018 and 2021 — 84 million tonnes in 2021 alone — on a stated trajectory toward 800 million tonnes by 2025.[13][14] Corporate Knights' 2025 assessment specifically credited the company's success in severing energy consumption and carbon emissions from business growth.[10]

7.2 Social performance

Social indicators advanced in parallel: gender diversity of executives and board — explicitly cited by Corporate Knights in the 2025 ranking[10] — alongside equal-pay programmes, safety performance, and energy-access initiatives moving toward the 50-million-person target. Multi-year engagement scores and the confidence-to-report-unethical-conduct index (81%) evidence the trust pillar internally.[14]

7.3 Governance and external ratings

7.4 Financial performance

Critically for RQ2, ESG leadership coincided with — rather than traded off against — financial outperformance. FY 2024 set company records: €38.2 billion revenue (≈+8% organic), €7.1 billion adjusted EBITA at an 18.6% margin, €4.3 billion net profit (+7%) and €4.2 billion free cash flow, with guidance for continued double-digit earnings growth.[11][15] The internal SSI score reached 7.55/10 in 2024, ahead of plan on the path to its 2025 endpoint.[11]

Section 08Discussion

Answering RQ1, the case shows digital transformation operationalised as an instrumentation layer: EcoStruxure sensing, edge control and cloud analytics make ESG variables continuously visible, while digital twins allow optimisation before capital is committed. This conforms to Kane et al.'s[3] finding that strategy drives transformation — the technology served a pre-existing sustainability thesis rather than generating one.

For RQ2, the evidence supports a dual dividend consistent with the meta-analytic literature.[5][6] Deep, long-horizon integration — as opposed to peripheral reporting — accompanied both the strongest ESG ratings in the sector and record financials in 2024. The flywheel in Figure 1 summarises the causal loop the evidence suggests: instrumentation enables credible disclosure; credible disclosure lowers the cost of capital (the SSI-indexed €2.7 billion facilities) and sharpens incentives; incentives accelerate delivery; delivery differentiates the product, expanding the very impact revenues that funded the instrumentation.

Culturally, the "Impact Company" framing resolved the classic strategy-versus-responsibility tension by declaring them identical — an existence proof of Eccles et al.'s[6] high-sustainability archetype operating at €38 billion scale.

Section 09Challenges and Limitations

The case is not frictionless. Industrial Automation contracted 4% in 2024 even as Energy Management grew 12%, illustrating cycle exposure across segments;[11] Scope 3 supplier decarbonisation remains the hardest target class and depends on thousands of counterparties;[13] and sustainability-linked finance raises verification stakes. Methodologically, the study relies substantially on company disclosure mitigated — but not eliminated — by third-party ratings; and single-case findings generalise analytically, not statistically.[1]

Section 10Recommendations

For RQ3, the transferable sequence for incumbents is: (1) Instrument — sensorise material ESG flows before setting targets; (2) Integrate — publish one quantified scorecard on a business cadence (quarterly), not an annual corporate-responsibility supplement; (3) Incentivise — bind executive pay and debt pricing to the scorecard to make slippage expensive; (4) Extend — carry the programme into the supply chain, where most impact sits; and (5) Monetise — convert proven internal capability into customer offerings so sustainability funds itself. For Schneider specifically, priorities include scaling AI-driven energy optimisation across mid-market customers, deepening supplier-level measurement, and extending circular (green-materials) metrics toward 100%.

Section 11Conclusion

Schneider Electric's two-decade trajectory demonstrates that digital transformation and ESG are not parallel programmes but a single reinforcing system. Digital infrastructure supplied the measurement, optimisation and verification machinery; ESG strategy supplied the objectives; governance and financial engineering supplied enforcement. The result — the industry's top ESG ratings, two Corporate Knights crowns, and record 2024 financials — constitutes a documented case in which improving sustainable strategies improved performance. The lesson for industrial incumbents is less "adopt ESG" than "wire it in": what gets continuously measured gets managed, and what gets priced gets done.
